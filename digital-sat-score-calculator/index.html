Type how many questions you got right in each module, pick the practice test you sat, and the tool runs the official College Board conversion table for that exact form. What comes back is a score range rather than a single number, because the tool reports both ends of the band its table gives, plus how wide that band is. Every result here is UNOFFICIAL.

What the numbers mean

College Board reports an SAT total score on a fixed published range, and the two section scores, Reading and Writing and Math, each sit on their own published range.

Why your estimate is a band and not a point

The SAT Suite uses a multistage adaptive design. Each test section, Reading and Writing and Math, is divided into two equal-length, separately timed modules, and the mix of questions in the second module is on average either of higher difficulty or of lower difficulty than the mix in the first. Scoring runs on Item Response Theory methodology alongside that design. So on the real test, two students who answer the same number of questions correctly in a test section may earn differing section scores. The tool hands back both ends of the band and its width instead of picking a point inside it and calling that your score.

The two percentiles, and which one to read

Every estimate here carries both percentile groups College Board publishes. Nationally Representative Sample percentiles are weighted to represent all U.S. students in 11th and 12th grade. User Group percentiles are based on the actual SAT scores of students who graduated in the past three school years. Same score, two comparison groups, two numbers, and the tool prints both for the bottom and the top of your range. At the top of the scale College Board prints the censored label 99+ rather than a plain number, and the tool prints that label exactly as published.

Where these tables come from

Every score the tool shows comes out of a conversion table transcribed from an official College Board scoring guide, version stamped with that form's label, copyright line and document code, and shown next to your result. I transcribed each loaded form from its own guide and checked the shape of the table before wiring it in, so a missing row or a broken step would surface before a student ever saw it. Then I built the refusal path first. A raw score the table does not cover returns estimate unavailable, because a guessed cutoff is worse than no answer. Nothing here is remembered by a model, and your inputs never leave the page, since the arithmetic runs in your browser where you can read it.

Questions readers actually ask

Which practice tests can I score here?
The eight forms in the selector, Practice Test #4 through Practice Test #11. Each carries its own conversion table, and the coverage line above the selector names them.
Why a range instead of one score?
Because the table gives a range. Each raw score maps to a lower and an upper section score, so the tool adds the lower ends together and the upper ends together, then shows how many points wide the band is.
I sat the real SAT, not a paper practice test.
Nothing here converts that one. College Board releases SAT scores to students in an online score report in their College Board account.
Which percentile should I read?
Read both. Each compares you against a different population, and the tool prints them side by side for the bottom and the top of your range.
The table shows a lower bound that dips as the band widens. Is that a mistake?
Not on our side. Where a loaded form has a row like that, the footnote under the table flags it, and the page ships the value College Board printed without smoothing it.
Does a strong estimate mean I get in somewhere?
No. A score estimate does not guarantee admission, credit, placement, or any outcome, and it is never your official score.
